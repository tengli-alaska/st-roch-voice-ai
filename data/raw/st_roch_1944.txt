The St. Roch and the 1944 Northwest Passage Voyage

The RCMP schooner St. Roch completed the Northwest Passage in a single season in 1944, traveling from Halifax to Vancouver in just 86 days between July 22 and October 16. This was the first time any vessel had completed the passage in a single season, and it took the more northerly route through Lancaster Sound and Barrow Strait, considered the true Northwest Passage.

The ship was commanded by Sergeant Henry Larsen, a Norwegian-born RCMP officer who had spent many years in the Canadian Arctic. The crew consisted of 11 other RCMP members fulfilling duties as both policemen and sailors.

Joe Panipakuttuk and the Inuit Guides

Joe Panipakuttuk was an Inuit guide and hunter whose contributions were critical to the success of the 1944 voyage. Joe, his wife Letia, their children, and other family members traveled with the crew for part of the voyage. Because the crew quarters were limited, the Inuit family lived in a tent on the deck of the ship.

Joe and his eldest son Aariak were praised by Larsen as natural seamen and skilled hunters. They guided the ship through treacherous ice conditions and hunted for fresh meat to feed the crew. In 1974, Joe Panipakuttuk was posthumously awarded the Polar Medal, recognizing his invaluable contributions to the success of the 1944 voyage.

A woman named Panikpak also contributed essential navigation knowledge. One crew member later remembered: "One time in the Passage we weren't sure where we were. Even Larsen was unsure. Then the Eskimo woman looked at the coastline and checked with the chart. That is where we are, she said. And that is where we were."

Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit and the Voyage

Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ) is traditional Inuit knowledge that encompasses ways of thinking, knowing, being, and interacting with the world. Its principles include respect and care, serving and providing for family and community, decision-making through discussion and consensus, and respect and care for the land, ice, and water.

The crew of the St. Roch relied on these principles during their time in the Arctic, and on the Inuit communities guided by them. Every expedition into the North had an entire community and a knowledge base supporting it. The success of the 1944 voyage depended on Inuit knowledge of ice conditions, navigation, hunting, and survival in the Arctic.

The Ship Itself

The St. Roch was built in 1928 at Burrard Dry Dock in North Vancouver. It was constructed primarily of thick Douglas fir planks reinforced with heavy beams to withstand ice pressure, with an outer shell made of Australian eucalyptus ironbark, one of the hardest woods in the world. The ship was 104 feet long and displaced 323 tons.

Between 1928 and 1954, the St. Roch logged tens of thousands of miles in the Arctic, acting as a floating detachment of the RCMP. It served as a supply ship, patrol vessel, and transport, and was often the only link between scattered northern communities.

The St. Roch was the second vessel to transit the Northwest Passage, after Roald Amundsen's Gjoa in 1903. It was the first to make the voyage from west to east (1940-1942), the first to complete it in a single season (1944), and later the first to circumnavigate North America (1950).

The ship is now a National Historic Site of Canada, permanently berthed at the Vancouver Maritime Museum since 1962.

RCMP Role in the Arctic

The Royal Canadian Mounted Police used the St. Roch to maintain Canadian sovereignty over Arctic territory. RCMP duties in the Arctic included enforcing Canadian laws, selling hunting licenses, collecting customs duties and taxes, delivering mail, registering vital statistics, making government payments, delivering supplies to isolated outposts, transporting the sick and injured to hospital, and transporting Inuit children to and from residential schools.

When the ship was wintered-in in the frozen ice, the crew would revert from being sailors to being regular RCMP constables, making winter patrols and performing police duties such as investigating deaths and taking a census of the Inuit population.
